Six bands, one for each stage below.

April, year one

It arrives from the hatchery the size of a lentil, a Pacific rock oyster, Magallana gigas, 4 mm from hinge to lip. We put it in the water on the same tide it lands.

4 mm long, under 0.1 g

May, year one

We tip the seed into black mesh bags with 4 mm holes and strap them to steel trestles low on the foreshore. Twice a day the tide covers them and twice a day they sit in the air, which is what hardens the shell.

9 mm long, 0.4 g

July to October, year one

On every spring tide we walk row C and flip each bag. The shaking chips the soft new lip, so the oyster builds a deep cup instead of a long flat shell, and weed and silt fall off with it.

28 mm long, about 4 g

November to March

Below 10 °C an oyster barely feeds. The band it lays down over winter is the thinnest and darkest on the shell, so you can count the winters on any oyster we send you.

31 mm long, about 5 g

Year two

Each spring the whole crop goes through a grader. The big ones move to 14 mm mesh bags at half the density, and the slow ones go back for another round. C-214 changed bags three times.

62 mm long, 38 g

September, year three

At about 90 grams it is ready. We lift the bags on a low tide, then rest the oysters for 42 hours in tanks of UV-cleaned seawater so they purge whatever they filtered from the estuary.

88 mm long, 92 g

The week it reaches you

We pack them cup side down so they keep their liquor, and date each box with the day it left the tanks. Keep them in the fridge under a damp cloth, never in water, and open them within five days.

Size 2, 88 mm, 92 g
